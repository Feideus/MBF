THE SIGNAL AT QUAI DES BRUMES
An original short story, written as placeholder/example content for the
Audio-Synced Reading Platform (MBF project). Not a public-domain literary
work — see note in README.md for why this replaces Albert Camus'
"L'Étranger" (still under copyright) as the platform's demo text.


I. THE STATION AT DUSK

The rain had been falling over Le Havre since noon, a fine grey rain that
softened the edges of the cranes along the harbor and turned the gas lamps
into smudges of amber. Inspector Aurélien Vasseur stood beneath the
awning of the Quai des Brumes station, collar up, watching the last
fishing boats come in low against the tide.

He had been a policeman for twenty-two years, long enough to know that a
telegram arriving after midnight rarely brought good news, and long
enough not to be surprised when it didn't. The telegram in his coat
pocket said only: COME AT ONCE. MARGUERITE IS MISSING. THE HOUSE ON RUE
DES SAULES.

He did not know a Marguerite. He had never been to Rue des Saules. And
yet the telegram bore his name, correctly spelled, and the signature of
a man he had buried nine years before.

The station clock read a quarter past eleven. Somewhere out past the
breakwater, a foghorn sounded, low and patient, as if the sea itself
were clearing its throat before speaking.


II. THE HOUSE ON RUE DES SAULES

The house was the kind that had once been grand and had since given up
pretending. Ivy had taken the north wall entirely; one shutter hung by a
single hinge and knocked against the stone whenever the wind turned.
Vasseur knocked twice before a woman opened the door, a lamp in one hand,
her other hand pressed flat against the frame as though she needed it to
stay upright.

"You came," she said. It was not a greeting so much as a verdict.

"I received a telegram signed by a dead man," Vasseur said. "I came to
find out who sent it."

"That would be me." She stepped back to let him in. "The signature was
the only way I knew to make you listen. My husband spoke of you often.
He said you were the only honest man left in this prefecture."

"Your husband is nine years in the ground, madame."

"I know precisely how long my husband has been dead, Inspector." Her
voice did not rise, but something in it sharpened, like a blade drawn
once across a stone. "It is my daughter who is missing. Marguerite. She
is nineteen. She went to the harbor market yesterday morning and did not
come home."

Vasseur removed his hat and turned it once in his hands, a habit from
younger days that still returned to him when a room asked more of him
than he was ready to give.

"Then let us not waste the hour on how you found my name," he said.
"Tell me about your daughter."


III. WHAT THE FISHWIFE SAW

The market smelled of brine and diesel and the particular sourness of
fish left too long in the sun before noon. Vasseur worked the stalls
methodically, the way he worked everything, starting at the harbor mouth
and moving inland, stall by stall, until an old woman selling mussels
from a tin basin stopped scaling a fish mid-motion and looked at him with
an attention he recognized as fear wearing the mask of caution.

"You're asking about the Verrier girl," she said. It was not a question.

"I am."

"There was a man." She set down her knife. "Tall, city clothes, gloves
though it wasn't cold enough for gloves. He'd been asking around the
stalls for three days running — who delivers to the Verrier house, what
time the girl comes to market, whether she comes alone." The woman
lowered her voice though there was no one near enough to hear. "Yesterday
he didn't ask. He waited by the mooring posts, there, where the Calypso
ties up. And when she passed him, he said one word to her, and she went
white as that gull's belly, and she went with him. Walked, not dragged.
Walked like a woman who'd been told a debt was due."

"What word?"

The fishwife hesitated, and in that hesitation Vasseur felt the case
turn over beneath him like a boat catching a swell.

"She said it back to him before she left. Only one word. 'Cassandre.'"

Vasseur went very still.

It was the name of a ship that had gone down in the Channel nine years
before, in the same week, by the same tide, that carried a man he had
once called a friend to the bottom of the sea — a man whose signature
now sat folded in his coat pocket, written in a hand that had not
trembled in nine years because it had not, in fact, been idle.


IV. RUNNING THE QUAY

He did not remember deciding to run. He remembered only that his legs
were carrying him along the quay before his mind had finished the
thought, boots striking wet stone, the rain turned suddenly to something
harder, more deliberate, stinging where it caught his face. The
Calypso's mooring lines groaned against their cleats. Somewhere ahead, a
door slammed — the old customs shed, boarded these six years, its
padlock hanging open and wet in the lamplight.

He went in low and fast, the way the war had taught him, back against
the frame, breath held.

A single lamp burned inside, throwing the shed's ribs into long black
shadow. A girl sat bound to a chair — Marguerite, he was certain of it
before he was told, from the set of her jaw, so like her mother's — and
before her stood a man in city clothes and gloves, a man Vasseur had
last seen laid out in a coffin with the lid already half-closed.

"You look well for a dead man, Cassandre," Vasseur said.

The man turned unhurried, and smiled the smile Vasseur remembered from
the inquest nine years before, the smile of someone who had already
worked out every version of the conversation in advance.

"I look well," the man said, "because dying was the easiest lie I ever
told. Put the pistol down, Aurélien. I only came back to collect what
your testimony cost me. The girl is merely how I made certain you would
listen before I explained."


V. WHAT THE SEA GIVES BACK

They sat afterward in the customs shed while the storm wore itself out
against the harbor wall, Marguerite wrapped in Vasseur's coat, the man
who had called himself dead now sitting with his wrists bound in the
girl's abandoned rope, talking in the flat unhurried voice of someone
who has waited nine years to be believed.

He spoke of a ship's manifest that did not match its cargo, of a
customs inspector paid to look away and a policeman who would not, of a
drowning staged in a storm not unlike this one so that a debt and a
danger could both be buried at once. He spoke of watching his own funeral
from the hill above the cemetery, and of the particular loneliness of a
man who exists for no one, filed away in no register, owed nothing and
owing everything.

Vasseur listened the way he always listened, without comment, turning
his hat once in his hands, and when the man had finished he said only:

"You will tell the examining magistrate everything you have told me.
Twice, if he asks it twice." He rose, and offered the girl his arm
toward the door and the thinning rain. "The sea," he added, not quite to
either of them, "is a poor place to bury a debt. It always gives
something back."

Outside, the foghorn sounded once more, and this time it did not sound
like a warning. It sounded, Vasseur thought, almost like relief.


THE END
